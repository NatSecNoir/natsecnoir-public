The hearing examines the national-security, economic, and operational risks that the Chinese Communist Party’s policies pose to American companies, investors, supply chains, and data.

**What it does** The transcript records opening statements, witness testimony, and questions concerning forced technology transfer, intellectual-property theft, coercion, opaque or manipulated economic data, arbitrary enforcement, raids and detentions, party control of companies, and the vulnerabilities of Variable Interest Entity investments. Witnesses and Members discuss possible responses, including stronger disclosures, due diligence, scrutiny of Chinese affiliations and university funding, restrictions on sensitive technology and outbound investment, supply-chain reshoring and diversification, renewal of the Generalized System of Preferences, and alternatives to the Chinese logistics platform LOGINK. The hearing also considers the risks posed by Chinese military-civil fusion and investment in Chinese military or surveillance-linked companies.

**Who it affects** The discussion is directed at American companies operating or investing in China, pension and retirement-fund investors, banks, asset managers, small and large technology and industrial firms, universities, ports, shipping and logistics providers, and policymakers overseeing finance, trade, technology, and national security. It also addresses allies and foreign companies whose operations or data may connect to Chinese systems.

**Why it matters** The hearing’s central warning is that commercial access in China remains subject to political control and may expose U.S. capital, technology, personal information, and critical supply chains to coercion or appropriation. It presents resilience, transparency, and selective risk reduction—not an immediate, across-the-board withdrawal—as the principal policy challenges.

**Key dates and numbers**
- Hearing held July 13, 2023; adjourned at 9:10 p.m.
- Witnesses testified that more than 60,000 Chinese economic indicators were discontinued and that nearly 7 trillion yuan in 2019 activity was erased from reported statistics.
- The transcript discusses a potential annual U.S. economic cost of intellectual-property theft as high as $600 billion.
