The report holds that China is using ostensibly civilian and commercial space cooperation in Latin America to build a dual-use infrastructure network that advances the People’s Liberation Army’s space-domain awareness, intelligence collection, and counterspace capabilities.

**What it does** The Select Committee reviews Chinese planning documents, open-source research, and satellite imagery concerning ground stations, radio telescopes, and satellite laser-ranging facilities in Argentina, Venezuela, Bolivia, Chile, and Brazil. It links these sites to China’s broader “Belt and Road” Spatial Information Corridor, Military-Civil Fusion policies, the Chinese space program, and PLA-linked organizations. The report explains how telemetry, tracking, command, radio-frequency collection, and data-processing capabilities could support surveillance, electronic warfare, missile operations, and targeting. It recommends NASA review relevant cooperation, Congress amend the Wolf Amendment, agencies condition cooperation on risk mitigation, the United States seek to roll back threatening infrastructure, and the United States expand civilian alternatives.

**Who it affects** The analysis concerns the United States and its allies, Latin American host governments, Chinese state and commercial entities, space agencies, defense organizations, and operators of affected facilities. It identifies implications for U.S. military space systems, communications, navigation, intelligence, and force mobilization.

**Why it matters** The report argues that geographically dispersed facilities can close surveillance and targeting gaps, provide near-global coverage, and create long-term strategic dependencies even when presented as scientific or civilian projects. Its conclusions are the Select Committee’s assessment and include potential uses rather than findings that every facility is being used militarily.

**Key dates and numbers**
- The report is dated February 26, 2026.
- It identifies at least 11 PRC-linked space facilities across five Latin American countries.
- The Neuquén station began operating in 2018 under a 50-year lease signed by Argentina and China.
- The report discusses Chinese space-policy documents from 2008, 2016, 2021, and 2025, and a China-CELAC action plan for 2025–2027.
- It describes five policy recommendations, including updating the Wolf Amendment.
