The House Select Committee on the CCP’s interim findings conclude that Temu lacks meaningful systems to prevent forced-labor goods from entering the United States and that Temu and Shein make extensive use of the de minimis import provision.

**What it does** The report presents preliminary results from the Committee’s investigation into Nike, Adidas, Shein, and Temu, which began with information requests sent on May 2, 2023. It focuses on the relationship between direct-to-consumer shipping, the Section 321 de minimis exception, and compliance with the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA). The Committee states that Temu reported no audits or affirmative compliance system, relying instead on seller terms prohibiting forced labor and an external complaint process. It also highlights Temu’s admission that it does not expressly prohibit third-party sellers from selling products based on origin in Xinjiang.

**Who it affects** The findings concern Temu, its roughly 80,000 sellers, Shein, Chinese suppliers, U.S. consumers, importers, and U.S. customs enforcement. Nike and Adidas are identified as companies that received information requests, although the report’s detailed findings primarily address Temu and Shein.

**Why it matters** The report argues that low-value direct shipments provide less customs data, avoid duties, and reduce the likelihood of screening for forced-labor indicators. It warns that the scale of these shipments may allow goods connected to Uyghur forced labor to enter U.S. commerce despite the UFLPA’s presumption against covered goods.

**Key dates and numbers**
- May 2, 2023: Committee information requests sent to four companies.
- September 2022: Temu launched in the United States.
- $800: U.S. de minimis value threshold discussed in the report.
- 685 million: de minimis shipments cleared by CBP in 2022.
- More than 30%: estimated share of daily U.S.-bound de minimis packages attributed to Temu and Shein together.
- About 80,000: sellers reported on Temu’s platform.
