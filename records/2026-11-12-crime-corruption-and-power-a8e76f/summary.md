The report finds that CCP-linked transnational criminal networks have formed a distributed regional ecosystem in which cyber-enabled fraud, human trafficking, corruption, selective enforcement, illicit finance, and dual-use infrastructure reinforce one another, creating material harms to Americans and U.S. national-security interests.

**What it does** The House Select Committee on the CCP examines cases in Cambodia, Burma, the Philippines, Pacific Island states, and emerging locations in Africa, Latin America, South Asia, and the Middle East. It develops a three-part framework—passive tolerance, instrumental enablement, and strategic exploitation—to distinguish forms of PRC- or CCP-linked complicity without assuming centralized direction from Beijing. The report documents how scam compounds and related networks use casinos, real estate, special economic zones, telecommunications, digital platforms, underground banking, crypto brokers, shell companies, and identity programs. It recommends legislation, sustained interagency coordination, sanctions and other pressure on protection networks, partner resilience, allied cooperation, private-sector coordination, and narrowly targeted cooperation with the PRC.

**Who it affects** Americans lose more than $10 billion annually to Southeast Asia–based scam networks, according to the report, while trafficked workers from more than 80 countries are forced to conduct fraud. U.S. agencies, financial institutions, technology and connectivity companies, allies, and vulnerable partner governments face exposure to fraud, laundering, sanctions evasion, governance erosion, and strategic access risks. The report gives particular attention to Chen Zhi and Prince Holding Group, Cambodian and Burmese protection networks, Philippine POGO infrastructure, and vulnerabilities in Pacific microstates.

**Why it matters** The report argues that enforcement can displace rather than destroy criminal capacity, allowing networks to relocate and reconstitute. It therefore treats the problem as a national-security convergence—not solely consumer fraud, cybercrime, trafficking, or organized crime—and emphasizes distributed resilience. It also cautions that available evidence does not establish a single CCP command structure or conclusively prove strategic exploitation in every case.

**Key dates and numbers**
- January 7, 2026: Chinese authorities took custody of Chen Zhi; October 14, 2025: U.S. authorities announced a $15 billion bitcoin seizure and related actions.
- More than $10 billion in annual losses to Americans; Cambodia estimates include upwards of 150,000 trafficked people and $12.5–$19 billion in annual illicit proceeds.
- 2019: Cambodia’s online-gambling ban; 2021: Burma’s military coup; 2024: the Philippines announced the termination of POGO operations.
- The report recommends action on H.R. 5490, S. 2950, and seven policy priorities.
