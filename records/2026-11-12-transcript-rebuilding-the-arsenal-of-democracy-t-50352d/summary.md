The hearing examines how the United States can rebuild its defense industrial base and workforce to restore deterrence and compete with the People’s Republic of China.

**What it does** The transcript records opening statements, witness testimony, and member questioning on production capacity, critical-mineral and component supply chains, procurement reform, commercial contracting, workforce development, allied industrial cooperation, and the use of autonomous systems and artificial intelligence. Witnesses Chris Brose, William Greenwalt, and Halimah Najieb-Locke argue that the United States should combine legacy-system investment with lower-cost, mass-producible autonomous vehicles and weapons; use purchase commitments, multiyear procurement, Defense Production Act and IBAS authorities, other transactions, and commercial contracting; and strengthen domestic and allied sources for castings, forgings, munitions, and critical minerals.

**Who it affects** The discussion concerns Congress, the Department of Defense, defense primes and suppliers, commercial technology companies, investors, manufacturing institutes, workers, and U.S. allies. It highlights vulnerabilities created by dependence on Chinese inputs, including gallium, antimony, germanium, castings, and forgings.

**Why it matters** The witnesses warn that current acquisition timelines, continuing resolutions, workforce shortages, and limited production capacity could leave the United States unable to sustain a high-intensity conflict. They urge faster, outcome-focused procurement and a stronger demand signal so industry can expand capacity during this decade.

**Key dates and numbers**
- Hearing held December 5, 2024; questions for the record were due one week later.
- Testimony cited a risk of exhausting critical munitions in less than one week of war with China.
- The National Defense Industrial Strategy identifies four priorities for the next three to five years.
- Many missile systems discussed require roughly two years to produce.
