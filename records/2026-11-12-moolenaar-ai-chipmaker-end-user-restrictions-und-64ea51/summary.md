The House Select Committee on the CCP urges the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) to clarify that its Foundry Due Diligence Rule remains in effect despite the decision not to enforce the Biden administration’s AI Diffusion Rule.

**What it does** The August 10, 2026, letter from Chairman John R. Moolenaar to Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler describes a potential gap in export-control guidance for front-end fabricators and outsourced semiconductor assembly and test companies. It asks BIS either to issue targeted guidance confirming that the worldwide regional-stability license requirement applies to exports from front-end fabricators or to rescind the AI Diffusion interim final rule while amending §744.23 to preserve a separate worldwide license requirement. Moolenaar also requests a staff-level briefing on the issue and related investigations.

**Who it affects** The requested clarification would principally affect advanced semiconductor foundries and OSATs exporting chips fabricated at the 14/16-nanometer node or below, as well as chip designers and end users subject to U.S. export controls. The letter discusses TSMC, Huawei, and Sophgo as examples of the risks posed by misrepresented end users and diverted chips.

**Why it matters** The committee argues that ambiguity could allow restricted AI chips to reach Chinese entities or their front companies, undermining controls intended to preserve U.S. technological advantages. It cites Huawei’s acquisition of advanced Ascend logic dies through Sophgo and warns of another similar diversion if foundries conclude that the due-diligence rule is no longer enforceable.

**Key dates and numbers**
- January 2025: Foundry Due Diligence Rule established.
- May 2025: BIS announced it would not enforce the AI Diffusion Rule.
- October 2024: TechInsights reported TSMC-manufactured chiplets in Huawei processors.
- August 31, 2026: requested deadline for the BIS staff briefing.
- 14/16 nanometers or below: fabrication threshold discussed in the rule.
