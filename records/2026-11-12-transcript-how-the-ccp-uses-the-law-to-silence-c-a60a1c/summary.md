The hearing examines allegations that the Chinese Communist Party and PRC-linked entities exploit U.S. courts, legal systems, and commercial relationships to intimidate critics, protect sensitive information, and advance broader national-security objectives.

**What it does** The transcript records opening statements and witness testimony on alleged Chinese “lawfare,” including defamation threats and lawsuits against researchers and commentators, efforts to enforce Chinese judgments abroad, anti-suit injunctions, restrictions on discovery under PRC law, and claims of sovereign immunity. Witnesses also discuss PRC legal and regulatory tools affecting data, technology, trade, maritime activity, and political speech. The hearing presents possible congressional responses, including heightened pleading standards, anti-SLAPP protections, support for targeted researchers, greater transparency, and a proposed reporting role for the Department of Defense under section 1284 of the Senate draft 2025 NDAA.

**Who it affects** The discussion focuses on researchers, universities, media organizations, U.S. companies, intellectual-property holders, and litigants facing PRC-based counterparties. Examples include Anna Puglisi’s account of legal threats from BGI and MGI, litigation involving BYD and YMTC, and an anti-suit injunction involving Oppo and Sharp Electronics. ZPMC’s refusal to provide information to the committee is presented as a supply-chain and port-security concern.

**Why it matters** The witnesses argue that asymmetric access to information and courts can chill research, burden U.S. defendants, expose sensitive information, and influence technology, trade, and national-security decisions. Jill Goldenziel also cautions that more research is needed to distinguish coordinated PRC legal warfare from ordinary commercial litigation.
